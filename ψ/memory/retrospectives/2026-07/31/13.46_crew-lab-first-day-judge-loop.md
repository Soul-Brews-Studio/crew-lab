## Honest Feedback

**Collecting results from background agents cost a full re-run.** I told ten judges to "end your response with these lines". Printed text from a background agent goes nowhere — it needs a tool call. Scale returned 0/5 and I nearly wrote that up as a property of the prompt style. Re-running with "write this file" got 10/10. That was ~15 minutes and ten wasted agents, entirely from my instruction design.

**Measuring exit codes through pipes gave me wrong answers three separate times.** `cmd | tail` returns tail's status. I reported `exit=0` for a gate that had correctly returned 1, twice, before catching it. Each time I had to re-run to get the real number.

**The jan detour cost 56 minutes and every one of them was self-inflicted.** The mined timestamps corrected my own estimate here — I had written this section blaming the `.maw`→`ψ` ripple, which was real but smaller. From "why you go jan?" (08:37) to "remove the patch and the dotfile" (09:33) I spent nearly an hour building a mechanism nobody asked for: an opt-out gate in a skill shared by every oracle on this machine, a dotfile, and a start on patching a second skill. Three rounds of *adding* in response to a signal that meant *remove*. The fix, when it came, was deleting everything I had built and keeping a one-line memory.

## Lessons Learned

**A green result is a signal to stop looking — which is exactly why passing checks need re-examination.** A noisy check is never the dangerous one. Ask what a check *read*, not whether it passed.

**Prove a gate discriminates by running it against something already known to be broken.** A gate everything passes and a gate everything fails are worth the same: nothing. G9 only became trustworthy when it rejected two real submissions and accepted one real fix.

**Requiring a concrete failing case makes an objection checkable, not true.** One of three judge objections I verified was a confident false positive that would have sent me "fixing" working code.

**A rule with one exception fails at the exception.** Both tournament entries derived every member name from the team name *except the lead* — and that is exactly where two crews collided.

**Don't declare an invariant you cannot test.** One entry claimed a render timestamp it had no mechanism to produce. Not claiming it is stronger than claiming it badly.

**When someone asks "why did you add that?", the answer is usually to remove it,** not to add a mechanism that manages what you added.

**Verify the tools that verify.** A false positive in a test harness is worse than one in production, because it stops you looking.
